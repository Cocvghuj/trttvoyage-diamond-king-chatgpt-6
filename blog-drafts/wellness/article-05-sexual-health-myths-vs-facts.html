Common Sexual Health Myths vs Facts

Misinformation can create unnecessary worry and sometimes delay care. Understanding a few important facts can help you ask better questions and find reliable health information.

By: TRTT Voyage Editorial Team · Category: Sexual Health

Why checking the facts matters

Sexual health misinformation can make people anxious, encourage unsafe decisions or delay appropriate care. Health guidance should come from recognised public-health organisations and qualified healthcare professionals—not rumours, online comments or unsupported product claims.

Five common myths and the facts

Myth 1: You can always tell whether someone has an STI by looking at them.

Fact: Many sexually transmitted infections (STIs) may have no noticeable symptoms. A person’s appearance cannot confirm whether they have an infection. Testing and professional advice are more reliable.

Myth 2: No symptoms means there is no reason to ask about testing.

Fact: Some STIs can be present without obvious symptoms. Testing recommendations depend on personal circumstances, possible exposure, age, anatomy and local guidance. A healthcare professional can help decide which tests are appropriate and when to take them.

Myth 3: One test is automatically right for everyone.

Fact: Different infections may require different tests and sample sites. The appropriate approach can depend on the type and timing of possible exposure and individual circumstances. Explain your concerns honestly to the clinician so they can advise you.

Myth 4: Contraception and STI prevention are the same thing.

Fact: Pregnancy prevention and infection prevention are different health considerations. Some methods of contraception do not protect against STIs. Ask a qualified healthcare professional about options that fit your needs and circumstances.

Myth 5: Online remedies can replace diagnosis or treatment.

Fact: Home remedies and unverified products cannot reliably diagnose or treat an STI. Relying on them may delay appropriate care. Symptoms or possible exposure deserve professional assessment.

How to check sexual health information

  • Look for recognised public-health organisations, such as the CDC and WHO.
  • Check when the information was published or last reviewed.
  • Be cautious of products promising guaranteed cures or instant results.
  • Remember that recommendations can vary by country and personal circumstances.
  • Ask a qualified healthcare professional when information is unclear or relates to your own health.

When to seek professional advice

Speak with a qualified healthcare professional if you have symptoms, think you may have been exposed to an STI, or want advice about testing or prevention. They can explain which tests or services may be appropriate. Do not wait for symptoms if official local guidance or a clinician recommends testing after a possible exposure.

Frequently asked questions

Can an STI have no symptoms?

Yes. Some STIs may not cause noticeable symptoms. Testing is the way to check, with the right test and timing guided by a healthcare professional.

Does contraception always prevent STIs?

No. Pregnancy prevention and STI prevention are separate considerations. Ask a clinician about methods that address your needs.

Can I use an online symptom checker as a diagnosis?

No. Online information can help you prepare questions, but it cannot confirm an infection or replace professional assessment and testing.

Where can I find reliable STI information?

Start with recognised public-health sources such as the CDC and WHO, and consult a qualified healthcare professional for advice that fits your circumstances.
