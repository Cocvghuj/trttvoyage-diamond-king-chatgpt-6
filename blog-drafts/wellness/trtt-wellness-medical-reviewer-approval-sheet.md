## 1. Reviewer eligibility and independence

Request review from a currently licensed/registered healthcare professional whose scope of practice matches the material being reviewed. Depending on article scope, this may include a physician with relevant sexual/reproductive-health experience, a sexual-health clinician, a gynaecologist, urologist, reproductive-health specialist, or appropriately qualified public-health professional.

Before review, record and verify:
- Full professional name and preferred public attribution (only with permission).
- Profession, relevant specialty and scope of practice.
- Registration/licence body and registration number, verified through the official register where available. Keep private contact and licence details out of public-facing pages unless necessary and explicitly approved.
- Country/jurisdiction of registration and current status.
- Relevant experience with sexual health, STI prevention/testing, reproductive health or the other assigned topic.
- Review date, version/commit reviewed, conflicts of interest and any payment/relationship disclosure.
- Whether the reviewer approves medical content only, or also approves public-facing attribution. These are separate permissions.

Do not call someone a “doctor”, “medical reviewer” or “clinically approved” without verified credentials and explicit written permission to publish that attribution. Editorial review is not a substitute for qualified clinical review.

## 2. Review method and required evidence

For every clinical or health-related claim:
1. Identify the exact sentence/paragraph and article version.
2. Decide whether it is accurate, appropriately qualified, current and relevant to adult readers.
3. Check it against current authoritative guidance (for example WHO, CDC, NHS, ECDC, Italy's Ministry of Health / Istituto Superiore di Sanità, or the relevant national health authority). Use the authority appropriate to the claim and target country; do not treat one country's guidance as universal.
4. Record the exact source title, issuing organisation, URL, publication/update date (if shown), access date and the claim it supports.
5. Flag missing evidence, conflicting guidance, overgeneralisation, outdated information, misleading reassurance, unnecessary alarm, stigma or unsafe self-care implications.
6. Recommend an exact correction or deletion from the draft. The owner/editor must make and re-check corrections; reviewer sign-off applies only to the version identified in the approval record.
7. Re-review any later substantive medical edit. Approval does not automatically carry over to a changed file.

**Evidence standard:** Prefer current official public-health guidance, clinical guidelines and systematic evidence. Avoid unsupported commercial claims, influencer posts, forums, anonymous advice and AI-generated citations. If reliable evidence is unavailable or guidance varies, say so clearly and narrow the claim.

## 3. Cross-article clinical and safeguarding checklist

- [ ] Content is educational and does not diagnose an individual or prescribe personalised treatment.
- [ ] Medical terminology is accurate, defined in plain language and used consistently.
- [ ] Claims are supported by current, relevant, traceable sources.
- [ ] No absolute guarantee, cure promise, risk-free claim or universal “normal” standard is implied.
- [ ] Symptoms are described without implying that a reader can self-diagnose from a checklist.
- [ ] Advice about when/how urgently to seek care is proportionate and clinically appropriate.
- [ ] Testing windows, test types, sample sites, test limitations and follow-up are not oversimplified.
- [ ] Prevention statements distinguish pregnancy prevention from STI prevention and do not overstate protection.
- [ ] Medication, vaccination, contraception and treatment statements reflect current local guidance and eligibility differences.
- [ ] Pregnancy-related warning signs and urgent-care language are accurate and appropriately cautious.
- [ ] Privacy, confidentiality and access-to-care statements are not presented as identical in every country or service.
- [ ] Content respects differences in anatomy, age, disability, gender, relationship status and personal goals without stereotypes.
- [ ] No shame, coercion, victim-blaming or implication that a person is responsible for another person's abuse.
- [ ] Relationship counselling advice does not direct people experiencing coercion or violence into unsafe joint sessions; individual safety takes priority.
- [ ] Consent content is not presented as a substitute for jurisdiction-specific legal advice.
- [ ] The disclaimer is visible, but is not used to excuse inaccurate or unsafe claims.
- [ ] All reviewer-requested changes are closed or explicitly listed as unresolved blockers.

## 5. Country and service localization

The drafts are intended for an international audience, but healthcare access, confidentiality, age rules, testing services, emergency numbers, legal definitions and available treatments differ by country.

Before publication, choose and record the intended launch audience. At minimum, ask the reviewer/editor to check:
- **Italy / EU:** current official Italian/EU healthcare sources, local sexual-health service pathways, confidentiality and emergency-care wording.
- **Bangladesh:** only if Bangladesh is an intended target audience; verify current official national healthcare pathways and avoid assuming UK/Italian services apply.
- **United Kingdom:** the drafts currently link to NHS service information in Articles 07 and 10. Clearly label it as UK-specific and verify the exact linked service page still supports the wording.
- **Other countries:** add a country-specific review or keep advice general and direct readers to their own official health service.

Do not invent clinic addresses, phone numbers, emergency numbers, service availability, legal rights or age thresholds. If a local pathway cannot be verified, use a cautious general direction to consult the relevant official health service and leave country-specific claims out.
